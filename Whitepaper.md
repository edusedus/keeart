# keeart Taxonomies
## Fields of perception and the artistic opportunities of generative AI media

Generative AI media extend artistic practice by making perception itself a manipulable, explorable material. Rather than only representing a given scene or subject, these systems allow artists to model, perturb, and reconfigure the sensory and cognitive filters through which we experience imagery, sound, and motion. That capability opens several concrete artistic opportunities:

- **Expand perceptual vocabularies:** Generative models synthesize visual and auditory forms that lie between styles, eras, and sensory modalities. Artists can invent new textures, color logics, and sonic grammars that challenge learned categories (photorealism, illustration, ambient soundscape), enabling works that feel simultaneously familiar and novel.

- **Probe subjective seeing and hearing:** Prompting can foreground biases in human perception—how composition, lighting, scale, and framing guide attention. By systematically varying prompts (e.g., emphasis on edge contrast, microtexture, or spatial distortion), creators can produce series that reveal how tiny changes alter perceived mood, depth, or narrative, turning perception into an experimental variable.

- **Simulate alternate perceptual systems:** Models can be prompted to emulate nonhuman or hypothetical sensory apparatuses (insect compound-eye views, sonar-like abstraction, synesthetic mappings between color and pitch). These outputs let audiences inhabit alternative epistemologies and question anthropocentric assumptions about what counts as a legible image or sound.

- **Make processes visible:** Generative workflows—latent-space interpolation, style mixing, attention-based transformations—can be exposed as part of the artwork. Animated traversals through latent space or layered visualizations of model attention translate hidden AI operations into perceptual experience, inviting reflection on how machine “seeing” differs from human vision.

- **Create perceptual narratives at scale:** Procedural prompting enables the production of large, coherent variations on a theme. This supports long-form works (series, installations, VR environments) that explore gradual perceptual shifts—metamorphosis of form, progressive desaturation, morphing of spatial cues—so viewers can perceive transformation as a temporal dimension rather than a single snapshot.

- **Surface cultural and cognitive frames:** Generative outputs reflect training data and the conceptual conventions embedded in prompts. Artists can leverage this to interrogate collective visual imaginaries—how media, memory, and language shape perception—by contrasting model-generated variants that amplify, remove, or juxtapose culturally loaded features.

- **Foster participatory perception:** Interactive prompting interfaces let audiences co-create variations in real time, making perception a shared, performative act. This democratizes the exploration of sensory affordances and turns viewers into agents who discover contingencies in how images and sounds are constructed.

Taken together, these opportunities position generative AI not just as a new tool for making images and sounds but as a laboratory for perception.


## The relevance of prompting strategies in creative expression with generative AI

Prompting strategies are the practical grammar and methodology that translate artistic intent into algorithmic output. They shape creative possibilities, define aesthetic constraints, and mediate the relationship between human imagination and model behavior.

- **Prompting as compositional technique:** Prompts function like units of control that can be combined, repeated, and varied. Structured prompting (templates, staged prompts, weighted tokens) lets creators compose images and sounds with predictable affordances, enabling deliberate formal choices rather than accidental results.

- **Control over specificity and ambiguity:** Different strategies (highly detailed literal prompts vs. terse conceptual prompts; negative prompting; token weighting) let artists dial between precision and serendipity. Controlling the balance of specificity and openness is itself an expressive decision that affects surprise, nuance, and interpretive space.

- **Procedural and parametric creativity:** Strategies such as prompt chaining, iterative refinement, variable seeding, and latent-space interpolation create procedural systems for exploration. These enable reproducible series, controlled variation, and emergent behaviors—core methods for working at scale or in time-based media.

- **Encoding aesthetic rules and constraints:** Prompt taxonomies let artists externalize and reuse stylistic constraints (lighting recipes, lens/camera conventions, compositional heuristics). This codification supports consistent aesthetics across a body of work and provides a shared language for collaboration and critique.

- **Bridging conceptual intention and model biases:** Prompting is the interface where human goals meet model priors. Effective strategies anticipate and counteract dataset biases (e.g., representation imbalances, stereotyped associations) through framing, counter-prompts, or targeted conditioning—allowing artists to steer outcomes ethically and conceptually.

- **Tool for discovery and research-through-practice:** Systematic prompting is a research method: variations expose how models encode visual concepts, attention, and semantics. This generates insight into both technical behavior and creative affordances, informing iterative practice, pedagogy, and critical analysis.

- **Facilitating collaboration and reproducibility:** Well-documented prompting patterns act as recipes that other practitioners can adapt. They enable reproducible experiments, shared workshops, and cumulative improvement—turning individual exploration into communal knowledge.

- **Enabling new interaction paradigms:** Prompting strategies underpin interactive and generative systems where audiences influence outputs (live prompts, reactive narratives, adaptive installations). Strategy design determines how agency is distributed between human participants and the model.

Prompting strategies are the procedural language of generative art: they convert intention into artifacts, scaffold experimentation, and make creative practice legible and shareable. 


## The relevance of taxonomies as an intermediary format between machine-readable and human-readable classifications

Taxonomies act as protocol layers that translate between human creative intentions and machine-operational structures. For generative AI prompting—where aesthetic decisions, procedural patterns, and model behaviors intersect—taxonomies provide a shared, structured vocabulary that is simultaneously interpretable by people and actionable by systems. Their relevance can be described across several practical roles:

### 1. Standardizing descriptive practice
- **Consistency:** Taxonomies impose consistent labels and hierarchies for prompting techniques (e.g., “camera: low-angle,” “lighting: rim light,” “strategy: prompt chaining”), reducing ambiguity across practitioners.
- **Discoverability:** Standard descriptors make it easier to index, search, and compare prompts, assets, and outcomes in repositories and galleries.

### 2. Enabling computational tooling
- **Interoperability:** Machine-readable taxonomies (RDF, JSON-LD, SKOS) let tools parse, validate, and transform prompting metadata into parameters, templates, or pipeline steps.
- **Automation:** Structured tags support automated workflows—batch generation, variant synthesis, conditional branching in prompt chains—without losing the semantic nuance needed for creative control.

### 3. Preserving artistic intent
- **Explicit constraints:** Taxonomies codify stylistic rules and intent as discrete properties (tone, detail-level, affordance), enabling faithful reproduction and iterative refinement of aesthetic decisions.
- **Versioning & provenance:** Structured metadata captures how a prompt was constructed, which variations were tried, and which model/settings produced results—essential for reproducibility, attribution, and critique.

### 4. Facilitating collaboration and pedagogy
- **Shared language:** Taxonomies create an intelligible bridge for teams—artists, engineers, curators—to coordinate around the same concepts and methods.
- **Learning scaffolds:** Organized classifications help newcomers understand common strategies, tradeoffs, and patterns by grouping examples, best practices, and counter-examples.

### 5. Supporting critical and ethical practice
- **Bias auditing:** Systematic labels allow researchers to query and analyze how certain prompt types correlate with biased outputs, making mitigation and auditing tractable.

### 6. Enabling hybrid human–machine workflows
- **Human-in-the-loop:** Taxonomies let interfaces surface meaningful controls (e.g., toggles for “photorealism” vs “painterly abstraction”) that map directly to prompt templates or token weights.
- **Machine suggestions:** Models can consume taxonomy-backed examples to propose stylistically consistent prompts, autofill templates, or generate constrained variations that respect an artist’s intent.

### 7. Bridging granularity and abstraction
- **Multi-level representation:** Good taxonomies support both coarse labels for high-level discovery and fine-grained attributes for precise control, permitting workflows that range from exploratory browsing to strict replication.

Taken together, taxonomies function as an essential middleware: they make prompting legible to humans while structuring it for machines. This intermediary role amplifies creative agency (by clarifying and preserving intent), improves tooling (by enabling automation and interoperability), and strengthens social practices (by enabling shared vocabularies, reproducibility, and critical analysis). By encoding prompting knowledge in formats that machines can act on and humans can read, taxonomies convert ad hoc, tacit craft into durable, interoperable infrastructure for generative art.


## Why RDF

RDF (Resource Description Framework) provides an ideal backbone for representing prompting taxonomies because it blends semantic precision, interoperability, and extensibility—qualities essential for a shared infrastructure that must serve both human practitioners and automated tools.

- **Semantic clarity through triples:** RDF’s subject–predicate–object model makes relationships explicit (e.g., PromptA — usesStrategy — ChainedPrompt). That explicitness captures intent, provenance, and dependency in a way that’s machine-actionable and easy for humans to inspect.

- **Standardized vocabularies and reusability:** RDF interoperates with established semantic schemas (SKOS, Dublin Core, Schema.org). You can reuse and extend existing concepts (e.g., provenance, licensing, conceptual labels) rather than inventing ad hoc metadata fields, improving compatibility across platforms and communities.

- **Linked data & discoverability:** RDF is designed for linking entities across datasets. Prompt elements, style recipes, model versions, and example assets can reference widely used identifiers (URIs), enabling federated discovery, cross-referencing, and integration with external ontologies (color systems, camera ontologies, genre taxonomies).

- **Flexible granularity & modelling:** RDF handles multi-level descriptions naturally—simple tags for quick filtering or rich graphs for detailed procedural records (prompt chains, parameter graphs, attention maps). Blank nodes and reification let you represent complex metadata (ordered steps, weights, conditional branches) without breaking structure.

- **Machine-readability with human legibility:** RDF serializations (Turtle, JSON-LD) are both parseable by software and readable by humans. JSON-LD in particular (to be included at a later stage) maps cleanly to web-native tooling and developer workflows while preserving semantic rigor.

- **Provenance and traceability:** RDF integrates with provenance vocabularies (PROV-O), making it straightforward to record which model, seed, settings, and transformation steps produced an artifact—critical for reproducibility, credit, and auditing.

- **Querying and reasoning:** SPARQL enables powerful, semantically aware queries (find all prompts that use “rim light” and produced high-contrast outputs). RDF also supports lightweight reasoning/entailment, which can infer relationships (e.g., inheritance of stylistic constraints) to enhance tooling and recommendations.

- **Longevity and ecosystem support:** RDF is a W3C standard with mature tooling, libraries, and community practices. Choosing RDF leverages this ecosystem for validation, visualization, conversion, and integration in provenance-aware publishing or archival systems.

- **Policy and ethics mapping:** RDF graphs can encode rules and constraints that tooling can check (e.g., map prompt attributes to representation policies), enabling automated flagging and policy-aware generation without opaque heuristics.

In short, RDF turns prompting knowledge into linked, queryable, and extensible graphs that preserve artistic intent, enable interoperable tooling, and support reproducible, auditable creative workflows. For a project aiming to mediate between human-readable taxonomies and machine-driven generation, RDF is a pragmatic, future-proof choice.

## KeeArt Taxonomies: A series of RDF prompting taxonomies for generative AI media

KeeArt Taxonomies is a compact, open collection of RDF-encoded prompting taxonomies designed to map the practice of prompting for generative AI media. The project provides a foundational, interoperable vocabulary that both humans and machines can use to explore, reproduce, and extend creative workflows across visual, sonic, and multimodal domains.

### Purpose
- **Provide shared primitives:** Offer a baseline set of concepts (strategies, compositional attributes, lighting, camera conventions, style tokens) that artists, researchers, and toolmakers can adopt immediately.
- **Bridge human and machine use:** Encode classifications so they are human-legible and machine-actionable—supporting search, transformation into prompt templates, automated pipelines, and interactive interfaces.
- **Enable incremental growth:** Create an extensible scaffold that communities can extend with domain-specific terms, example galleries, and procedural recipes without breaking interoperability.

### What’s included
- RDF taxonomies for core prompting dimensions such as:
  - General prompting strategies (prompt chaining, negative prompting, weighting)
  - Lighting and color schemas
  - Camera angles, lenses, and framing conventions
  - Style and genre tags
- Example prompt instances, provenance metadata, and links to representative outputs (where licensing permits), serialized in JSON-LD and Turtle (upcomming).

### Key design principles
- **Interoperability:** Reuse established vocabularies (SKOS, PROV-O, Schema.org) and expose URIs for each term to enable federated linking.
- **Granularity:** Support both high-level discovery (browsing by strategy or aesthetic) and fine-grained control (parameterized templates, token weights).
- **Reproducibility:** Capture provenance (model version, seed, settings) so outputs can be consistently recreated or critically examined.
- **Extensibility:** Allow community contributions and domain ontologies to extend the core graphs while preserving backward compatibility.
- **Practicality:** Prioritize terms and structures that directly support tool integration—prompt builders, batch generators, and human-in-the-loop interfaces.

### Intended users and uses
- Artists and designers seeking reusable prompt recipes and clear labels for aesthetic intent.
- Developers integrating prompting controls into creative tools and APIs.
- Educators and researchers documenting prompting methods, running controlled experiments, and auditing model behavior.
- Curators and archivists preserving generative works with rich metadata for provenance and reproducibility.

### Outcomes and next steps
KeeArt aims to seed an ecosystem of interoperable prompt knowledge: searchable registries, prompt-authoring UIs that map human choices to RDF-backed templates, and analytic tools that surface correlations between prompting choices and model outputs. Future work includes expanding taxonomies, publishing exemplar datasets, and building reference implementations that demonstrate how RDF graphs translate into operational prompt pipelines.

KeeArt is a pragmatic starting point: a minimal, well-modeled RDF foundation that makes prompting legible, shareable, and extensible for both creative practice and tooling.
